    The one idea behind the whole course

    Barrett & Myers treat every imaging system the same way: the object is a vector \(\mathbf f\) in some space \(\U\), the image is a vector \(\mathbf g\) in some space \(\V\), and the imaging system is an operator \(\Hc\) that maps one to the other.

    \[ \mathbf g = \Hc\,\mathbf f \qquad(\text{plus noise later: } \mathbf g=\Hc\mathbf f+\mathbf n)\]
    Chapter 1 gives you the language for \(\U,\V,\Hc\). Chapter 2 gives you the delta function, which is how you probe \(\Hc\) one point at a time. Chapter 3 gives you the Fourier basis, which makes shift-invariant \(\Hc\) diagonal. Chapter 7 puts these together for real systems.

    The unit-circle picture you will use everywhere

    Euler's formula ties the complex exponential to the two trig functions. Picture \(e^{i\theta}\) as an arrow of length 1 on the unit circle. Its shadow on the horizontal axis is the cosine; its shadow on the vertical axis is the sine.

    \[ e^{i\theta}=\re{\cos\theta}+i\,\im{\sin\theta},\qquad \cos\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2},\qquad \sin\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}\]
    Read it as: a cosine is two counter-rotating phasors whose imaginary parts cancel; a sine is the same pair with the opposite sign on one of them, divided by \(i\) (a 90° rotation). This is exactly why \(\cos\) transforms to two delta functions at \(\pm\xi_0\).
    Tricks that save time
    • \(e^{i\pi}=-1,\ e^{i\pi/2}=i,\ e^{2\pi i n}=1\) for integer \(n\). Quarter turns are multiplications by \(i\).
    • \(|e^{i\theta}|=1\) always, so multiplying by a phase factor never changes a magnitude. This is why the shift theorem leaves \(|F(\xi)|\) alone.
    • \((e^{i\theta})^*=e^{-i\theta}\): conjugation reflects across the real axis.
    • \(e^{ia}-e^{-ia}=2i\sin a\) and \(e^{ia}+e^{-ia}=2\cos a\). Factor out the "midpoint" phase: \(e^{ia}-e^{ib}=e^{i(a+b)/2}\,2i\sin\frac{a-b}{2}\).
    Chapter 1

    Vectors and Operators

    Everything you know about arrows in 3D carries over to functions. A function is a vector with infinitely many components, one for each value of \(x\). Integrals play the role that sums play for ordinary vectors.

    1.1 Linear vector spaces

    A vector is anything you can add to another one and scale by a number. The collection of such things, closed under those two operations, is a linear vector space. Examples: \(N\)-tuples (\(\R^N\), \(\C^N\)), polynomials of degree \(\le N\), square-integrable functions.

    Metrics and norms

    A norm \(\|\mathbf f\|\) is a length. It must be \(\ge 0\) (zero only for \(\mathbf 0\)), scale as \(\|\lambda\mathbf f\|=|\lambda|\,\|\mathbf f\|\), and obey the triangle inequality. A norm gives a distance: \(d(\mathbf f_1,\mathbf f_2)=\|\mathbf f_1-\mathbf f_2\|\).

    (1.3)–(1.6) The \(L_p\) family
    \[\|\mathbf f\|_2=\Big(\sum_{n=1}^N|f_n|^2\Big)^{1/2},\quad \|\mathbf f\|_1=\sum_n|f_n|,\quad \|\mathbf f\|_p=\Big(\sum_n|f_n|^p\Big)^{1/p},\quad \|\mathbf f\|_\infty=\max_n|f_n|\]
    Only \(p=2\) comes from a scalar product. That one fact is why the whole book lives in \(L_2\): you get angles, projections, adjoints, and orthogonal bases for free.

    Completeness: Cauchy, Banach, Hilbert

    • Cauchy sequence: terms get arbitrarily close to each other. Every convergent sequence is Cauchy, but the limit might fall outside the space (e.g. rationals converging to \(\sqrt2\)).
    • Complete: every Cauchy sequence converges to something inside the space. \(\E^N\) is complete.
    • Banach space = complete normed space. Hilbert space = Banach space whose norm comes from a scalar product: \(\|\mathbf f\|=\sqrt{(\mathbf f,\mathbf f)}\).
    Scalar product rules (physics convention, used throughout B&M)
    \[(\mathbf f_1,\mathbf f_2)=(\mathbf f_2,\mathbf f_1)^*,\quad (\mathbf f_1,\lambda\mathbf f_2)=\lambda(\mathbf f_1,\mathbf f_2),\quad (\lambda\mathbf f_1,\mathbf f_2)=\lambda^*(\mathbf f_1,\mathbf f_2),\quad (\mathbf f,\mathbf f)\ge0\]
    (1.9) Euclidean \(\E^N\)
    \[(\mathbf f_1,\mathbf f_2)=\sum_{n=1}^N f_{1n}^*\,f_{2n}\]
    (1.10) Function space \(\L_2(\alpha,\beta)\)
    \[(\mathbf f_1,\mathbf f_2)=\int_\alpha^\beta\!dx\,f_1^*(x)\,f_2(x)\]
    Syntax · sum becomes integral

    Compare (1.9) and (1.10). The index \(n\) became the continuous variable \(x\), and \(\sum_n\) became \(\int dx\). Treat \(f(x)\) as "component number \(x\)". That analogy (\(\L_2\) as Euclidean space with infinitely many dimensions) is the engine of the entire book.

    (1.14) Schwarz inequality
    \[|(\mathbf f_1,\mathbf f_2)|^2\le\|\mathbf f_1\|^2\,\|\mathbf f_2\|^2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|\cos\theta|\le1\]
    Equality only when \(\mathbf f_2=\gamma\mathbf f_1\). So \((\mathbf f_1,\mathbf f_2)/(\|\mathbf f_1\|\|\mathbf f_2\|)\) is the cosine of the angle between two functions.

    Weighted spaces \(\L_2(\alpha,\beta;w)\) put a weight \(w(x)\ge0\) in the integral; \(\cos x\) is not in \(\L_2(-\infty,\infty)\) but is in \(\L_2(-\infty,\infty;e^{-x^2})\). Sobolev spaces put derivatives in the norm.

    Basis vectors and expansion coefficients

    (1.15)–(1.17)
    \[\mathbf f=\sum_n\alpha_n\mathbf u_n,\qquad(\mathbf u_m,\mathbf u_n)=\delta_{mn}\ \Longrightarrow\ \alpha_m=(\mathbf u_m,\mathbf f)\]
    The recipe: to get a coefficient, take the scalar product with that basis vector; orthonormality kills every other term. You will use this exact move to derive Fourier-series coefficients.

    An infinite-dimensional space is separable if a countable basis exists (\(n=1\dots\infty\)). \(\L_2(\alpha,\beta)\) is separable, even for infinite limits.

    1.1.6 Continuous bases

    On a finite interval the Fourier basis is \(u_n(x)=\frac1{\sqrt a}e^{2\pi inx/a}\). Let \(a\to\infty\) and the discrete index becomes a continuous label \(\nu\):

    (1.20)–(1.22)
    \[f(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d\nu\,F(\nu)\,u_\nu(x),\qquad F(\nu)=\int dx\,u_\nu^*(x)f(x),\qquad (u_\nu,u_{\nu'})=\delta(\nu-\nu')\]
    The Kronecker delta \(\delta_{mn}\) becomes the Dirac delta \(\delta(\nu-\nu')\). The basis function \(e^{2\pi i\nu x}\) is not itself square-integrable; it lies outside the space it spans.

    Every bounded linear functional on a Hilbert space is a scalar product with some fixed vector. That is why every linear detector reading looks like \(\int(\text{sensitivity})\times(\text{object})\).

    Rank of a matrix = number of linearly independent rows = number of independent columns, and \(R\le\min(M,N)\). It counts how many independent measurements you really made.

    Differential operators such as \(d/dx\) fit too; with generalized functions \(d/dx\) is the integral operator with kernel \(\delta'(x'-x)\).

    1.3 Hilbert-space operators

    • Linear: \(\Hc[\mathbf f_1+c\mathbf f_2]=\Hc\mathbf f_1+c\Hc\mathbf f_2\).
    • Bounded: \(\|\Hc\mathbf f\|<Q\|\mathbf f\|\). For linear operators, bounded ⇔ continuous. \(d/dx\) is unbounded (a jump has a delta-function derivative with infinite norm). All matrices are bounded.
    • Compact: turns any bounded sequence into one with a convergent subsequence. Compact ⇒ discrete eigenvalue spectrum.
    (1.33) Hilbert–Schmidt test for compactness
    \[\int\!\!\int dx\,dx'\,|h(x',x)|^2<\infty\]
    Exam fact: a convolution kernel \(h(x'-x)\) on \((-\infty,\infty)\) fails this test (the outer integral of a constant is infinite). Convolutions are not compact, so they have a continuous spectrum, which is why their eigenfunctions are labeled by a continuous frequency \(\xi\). Degenerate kernels \(\sum_j p_j(x')q_j(x)\) pass: \(e^{-x^2-x'^2}\) is compact, \(e^{-(x-x')^2}\) is not.

    Inverses and null spaces

    • Null vector: \(\Hc\mathbf f_{null}=\mathbf 0\) with \(\mathbf f_{null}\neq\mathbf 0\). Objects differing by a null vector give the same image.
    • One-to-one (injective) ⇔ no null space ⇔ a left inverse exists: \(\Hc_L^{-1}\Hc=\Ic_\U\).
    • Onto (surjective) ⇔ every \(\mathbf g\) is reachable ⇔ a right inverse exists: \(\Hc\Hc_R^{-1}=\Ic_\V\).
    • Both ⇒ invertible (bijective). Real-world imaging operators are always singular.

    Projectors and outer products

    (1.46), (1.57), (1.60)
    \[\Pc^2=\Pc,\ \ \Pc^\dagger=\Pc;\qquad \Pc=\sum_n\mathbf p_n\mathbf p_n^\dagger,\qquad \mathbf p\mathbf p^\dagger\mathbf f=(\mathbf p,\mathbf f)\,\mathbf p\]
    An outer product \(\mathbf b\mathbf a^\dagger\) is a matrix with entries \(b_m a_n^*\). Applied to \(\mathbf c\) it returns \(\mathbf b\) scaled by the number \(\mathbf a^\dagger\mathbf c\). In Dirac notation \(\mathbf b\mathbf a^\dagger=|b\rangle\langle a|\).
    (1.62) Closure / completeness · (1.64) continuous version
    \[\sum_n\mathbf u_n\mathbf u_n^\dagger=\Ic,\qquad\int d\nu\,u_\nu^*(x')u_\nu(x)=\delta(x-x')\]
    "Resolution of the identity." For the Fourier basis, \(\int d\nu\,e^{2\pi i\nu(x-x')}=\delta(x-x')\). You will meet this again as (2.46).

    Orthogonal complement: \(\Pc_{\mathbb S_\perp}=\Ic-\Pc_{\mathbb S}\).

    1.4 Eigenanalysis

    (1.65)
    \[\Hc\boldsymbol\psi=\lambda\boldsymbol\psi\iff(\Hc-\lambda\Ic)\boldsymbol\psi=\mathbf 0\]
    An eigenvector keeps its direction; only its length changes. \(\lambda\) is the shift that makes \(\Hc-\lambda\Ic\) singular. \(\lambda=0\) is an eigenvalue ⇔ \(\Hc\) is singular.
    • Discrete spectrum \(\lambda_n\) vs continuous spectrum \(\lambda_\nu\). Compact ⇒ discrete.
    • Similarity \(\mathcal W\Hc\mathcal W^{-1}\) keeps the spectrum; it is a change of coordinates. Unitary \(\mathcal W\) = rotation.
    • Finite matrices: \(\sum\lambda_n=\tr(\mathbf A)\), \(\prod\lambda_n=\det(\mathbf A)\); rank of a square matrix = number of nonzero eigenvalues; triangular ⇒ eigenvalues on the diagonal.
    Hermitian operators (the ones that matter)
    • Eigenvalues are real.
    • Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal; degenerate ones can be Gram–Schmidt'd.
    • \(\Hc^\dagger\Hc\) is always Hermitian and nonnegative-definite: \(\mu=\|\Hc\boldsymbol\phi\|^2/\|\boldsymbol\phi\|^2\ge0\).
    • Compact + Hermitian on a separable space ⇒ eigenvectors form a basis.
    (1.84)–(1.87) Diagonalization and spectral decomposition
    \[\boldsymbol\Psi^\dagger\mathbf A\boldsymbol\Psi=\boldsymbol\Lambda,\qquad \mathbf A=\sum_n\lambda_n\boldsymbol\psi_n\boldsymbol\psi_n^\dagger,\qquad \mathbf A^{-1}=\sum_n\frac1{\lambda_n}\boldsymbol\psi_n\boldsymbol\psi_n^\dagger\]
    \(\boldsymbol\Psi\) has the eigenvectors as columns, so \(\Psi_{mn}=\psi_{nm}\). A Hermitian operator is a weighted sum of 1D projectors, weights = eigenvalues.
    (1.90), (1.94) Continuous version
    \[a(x,x')=\int d\nu\,\lambda_\nu\,\psi_\nu(x)\psi_\nu^*(x'),\qquad[\boldsymbol\Psi^\dagger\Hc\boldsymbol\Psi](\nu,\nu')=\lambda_\nu\,\delta(\nu-\nu')\]
    "Diagonal" for a continuous index means the kernel is a delta function on the diagonal. Chapter 7 shows that Fourier transformation does exactly this to any convolution.
    1.4.6 Simultaneous diagonalization and prewhitening

    Commuting Hermitian matrices share eigenvectors, so one unitary \(\boldsymbol\Psi\) diagonalizes both. For non-commuting \(\mathbf A\) (positive-definite) and \(\mathbf B\), build \(\mathbf W=\boldsymbol\Psi\boldsymbol\Lambda_A^{-1/2}\boldsymbol\Phi\) so that \(\mathbf W^\dagger\mathbf A\mathbf W=\mathbf I\) and \(\mathbf W^\dagger\mathbf B\mathbf W=\mathbf D\). The middle step, \(\boldsymbol\Lambda_A^{-1/2}\boldsymbol\Psi^\dagger\mathbf A\boldsymbol\Psi\boldsymbol\Lambda_A^{-1/2}=\mathbf I\), is prewhitening (it will matter for noise covariances in Ch. 8). The columns of \(\mathbf W\) solve the generalized eigenproblem \(\mathbf B\mathbf W=\mathbf A\mathbf W\mathbf D\), i.e. are eigenvectors of \(\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf B\).

    1.5 Singular-value decomposition

    Most imaging operators are not Hermitian (often \(\U\ne\V\)), so we eigen-decompose the two Hermitian operators we can always build: \(\Hc^\dagger\Hc\) on \(\U\) and \(\Hc\Hc^\dagger\) on \(\V\).

    1. Solve \(\Hc^\dagger\Hc\,\mathbf u_n=\mu_n\mathbf u_n\) with \(\mu_1\ge\mu_2\ge\dots\ge\mu_R>0\). The \(\mathbf u_n\) are an orthonormal basis of object space.
    2. Get the image-space partners for free: \(\mathbf v_n=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_n}}\Hc\mathbf u_n\). These satisfy \(\Hc\Hc^\dagger\mathbf v_n=\mu_n\mathbf v_n\): the same eigenvalues.
    3. Write the operator as a sum of outer products (1.120):
    \[\Hc=\sum_{n=1}^R\sqrt{\mu_n}\;\mathbf v_n\mathbf u_n^\dagger,\qquad\Hc^\dagger=\sum_{n=1}^R\sqrt{\mu_n}\;\mathbf u_n\mathbf v_n^\dagger\]
    Read it as: take the component of \(\mathbf f\) along \(\mathbf u_n\), multiply by the singular value \(\sqrt{\mu_n}\), and lay it down along \(\mathbf v_n\). In SVD coordinates imaging is plain multiplication: \(\beta_n=\sqrt{\mu_n}\,\alpha_n\) where \(\alpha_n=\mathbf u_n^\dagger\mathbf f,\ \beta_n=\mathbf v_n^\dagger\mathbf g\).

    1.5.2 The four subspaces

    Object space \(\U\)

    Measurement space \(\U_{meas}=\mathcal N_\perp(\Hc)=\mathcal R(\Hc^\dagger)\), spanned by \(\mathbf u_1..\mathbf u_R\).
    Null space \(\U_{null}=\mathcal N(\Hc)=\mathcal N(\Hc^\dagger\Hc)\), spanned by \(\mathbf u_{R+1}\dots\). Dimension \(N-R\).
    \(\mathbf f=\mathbf f_{meas}+\mathbf f_{null}\).

    Image space \(\V\)

    Consistency space \(\V_{con}=\mathcal R(\Hc)=\mathcal R(\Hc\Hc^\dagger)\): images some object could produce.
    Inconsistency space \(\V_{incon}=\mathcal N(\Hc^\dagger)\): only noise lives here. Trivial if \(R=M\).
    \(\mathbf g=\mathbf g_{con}+\mathbf g_{incon}\).

    Common mix-up

    A zero vector has zero length. A null vector has nonzero length but zero image. And the singular value is \(\sqrt{\mu}\), not \(\mu\); \(\mu\) is the eigenvalue of \(\Hc^\dagger\Hc\).

    1.6 Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse

    (1.130) Penrose equations
    \[\Hc\Hc^+\Hc=\Hc,\quad\Hc^+\Hc\Hc^+=\Hc^+,\quad(\Hc\Hc^+)^\dagger=\Hc\Hc^+,\quad(\Hc^+\Hc)^\dagger=\Hc^+\Hc\]
    Any \(\Hc^\#\) satisfying the first is a generalized inverse ("1-inverse"). Satisfy all four and it is unique: the Moore–Penrose \(\Hc^+\).
    (1.131), (1.135) SVD form and limit form
    \[\Hc^+=\sum_{k=1}^R\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_k}}\,\mathbf u_k\mathbf v_k^\dagger=\lim_{\eta\to0^+}(\Hc^\dagger\Hc+\eta\Ic)^{-1}\Hc^\dagger\]
    Read it as: invert each nonzero singular value, leave the zero ones at zero (1.146): \(\lambda^+=1/\lambda\) if \(\lambda\ne0\), else \(0\).

    1.7 Pseudoinverses and linear equations

    Exact solution exists for every \(\mathbf g\) iff

    \(R=M\) (onto, \(\V_{incon}\) trivial, \(\Hc\Hc^\dagger\) invertible). Needs \(M\le N\). Right inverse: \(\Hc^\dagger(\Hc\Hc^\dagger)^{-1}\).

    At most one solution iff

    \(R=N\) (one-to-one, no null space, \(\Hc^\dagger\Hc\) invertible). Needs \(M\ge N\). Unique & exact for all \(\mathbf g\) only if \(R=M=N\).

    (1.194) Normal equation · (1.196) general least-squares solution
    \[\Hc^\dagger\Hc\,\hat{\mathbf f}=\Hc^\dagger\mathbf g,\qquad\hat{\mathbf f}_{LS}=\Hc^+\mathbf g+(\Ic-\Hc^+\Hc)\mathbf y\]
    The first term is a particular solution, the second an arbitrary null vector (like the homogeneous solution of an ODE). Set \(\mathbf y=0\) to get the minimum-norm least-squares solution \(\hat{\mathbf f}_{MNLS}=\Hc^+\mathbf g\). If \(R=N\): \(\hat{\mathbf f}_{LS}=(\Hc^\dagger\Hc)^{-1}\Hc^\dagger\mathbf g\).
    (1.205), (1.210) MNLS in SVD coordinates and noise amplification
    \[\hat\alpha_k=\frac{\beta_k}{\sqrt{\mu_k}}\ (k\le R),\qquad\hat{\mathbf f}_{MNLS}=\mathbf f_{meas}+\sum_{k=1}^R\frac{\gamma_k}{\sqrt{\mu_k}}\mathbf u_k\]
    "Inverse filtering." Small singular values blow up the noise component \(\gamma_k\). This is the central weakness of the pseudoinverse and the reason for regularization (Ch. 15).
    1.7.6 Landweber / SIRT and friends
    • \(\hat{\mathbf f}^{(k+1)}=\hat{\mathbf f}^{(k)}+\Hc^\dagger(\mathbf g-\Hc\hat{\mathbf f}^{(k)})\), from the Neumann series \([\mathbf I-\boldsymbol\Omega]^{-1}=\sum_j\boldsymbol\Omega^j\).
    • Converges if \(\mu_{max}<2\); otherwise scale \(\Hc'=C\Hc\) with \(|C|^2\mu_{max}<2\), then \(\Hc^+=C\,[\Hc']^+\).
    • Splitting \(\Hc^\dagger\Hc=\mathbf A-\mathbf B\): \(\mathbf A=\mathbf I\) → Landweber; \(\mathbf A=\) diagonal → Jacobi; \(\mathbf A=\) lower triangle → Gauss–Seidel = Kaczmarz = ART.

    1.8 Reproducing-kernel Hilbert space

    A space where evaluating a function at a point is a bounded linear functional: \((\mathbf h(x'),\mathbf f)_{rk}=f(x')\). For a compact positive-definite Hermitian \(\mathcal A\) with eigenpairs \((\lambda_n,\psi_n)\), the kernel is \(h(x,x')=\sum_n\lambda_n\psi_n(x)\psi_n^*(x')\) and the norm \(\|\mathbf f\|_{rk}^2=\sum_n|\alpha_n|^2/\lambda_n\) penalizes components with small \(\lambda_n\), so only smooth functions have finite norm. When all \(\mu_n=1\) the kernel is just a projector. Paley–Wiener space (bandlimited functions, kernel \(B\sinc(Bx)\)) is the example that returns in sampling theory (§3.5).

    The Dirac Delta and Other Generalized Functions

    The delta function is a mathematical point source. Its image through a system is the point spread function. There are three ways to understand it: an intuitive spike, the limit of ever-narrower functions, and (rigorously) a rule for what it does inside an integral.

    2.1 Distributions

    A distribution is a linear, continuous functional acting on well-behaved functions \(t(x)\). We always write it in Riesz form, and the kernel \(g(x)\) inside is the generalized function, defined only by what the integral returns:

    \[\Phi_g\{t(x)\}=\int_{-\infty}^\infty dx\;g(x)\,t(x)\]

    \[\int dx\,g'(x)\,t(x)\equiv-\int dx\,g(x)\,t'(x)\]
    This is how you differentiate things that have no ordinary derivative (steps, deltas). Move the derivative onto the test function and pick up a minus sign.
    Products are dangerous

    The product of two generalized functions need not exist. \([\delta(x)]^2\) is undefined. Products are safe if one factor is infinitely differentiable, or both are \(L_2\).

    (2.19)–(2.24) Definition and sifting
    \[\delta(x-x_0)=0\ (x\ne x_0),\qquad\int\delta(x-x_0)\,dx=1,\qquad\int_a^b f(x)\,\delta(x-x_0)\,dx=\begin{cases}f(x_0)&a<x_0<b\\0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\]
    Sifting: integrating against \(\delta(x-x_0)\) plucks out the value at \(x_0\). Equivalent shorthand: \(f(x)\delta(x-x_0)=f(x_0)\delta(x-x_0)\), so \(x\,\delta(x)=0\).
    (2.27)–(2.29) Even and scaling
    \[\begin{gathered}\delta(-x)=\delta(x)\\\delta(ax)=\frac{\delta(x)}{|a|}\\\delta(ax-x_0)=\frac1{|a|}\delta\!\Big(x-\frac{x_0}a\Big)\end{gathered}\]
    (2.33) Delta of a function
    \[\delta[g(x)]=\sum_n\frac{\delta(x-x_n)}{|g'(x_n)|},\qquad g(x_n)=0\]
    Worked example: \(\delta(x^2-a^2)\)

    Roots \(x=\pm a\); \(g'(x)=2x\), so \(|g'(\pm a)|=2|a|\). Therefore \(\delta(x^2-a^2)=\frac{1}{2|a|}[\delta(x-a)+\delta(x+a)]\). Check: \(\int e^{x}\delta(x^2-1)dx=\frac{e+e^{-1}}2=\cosh 1\).

    Dimensional analysis

    \(\int dx\,\delta(x)=1\) is dimensionless, so \([\delta(x)]=1/[x]\). If \(x\) is in cm, \(\delta(x)\) is in cm\(^{-1}\); in \(n\)D, \([\delta(\mathbf r)]=1/L^n\). Use this to catch missing \(|a|\) factors.

    2.2.2 Limiting representations (Dirac sequences)

    Any family \(\psi_k\) that is even, nonnegative, integrates to 1, and narrows as \(k\to\infty\) tends to \(\delta\) in the sense \(\lim\int f(x)\psi_k(x-x_0)dx=f(x_0)\) (a convolution).

    (2.37)–(2.41), (2.44)
    \[\begin{aligned}\delta(x)&=\lim_{\epsilon\to0^+}\tfrac1\epsilon e^{-\pi(x/\epsilon)^2}=\lim_{\epsilon\to0^+}\tfrac1\epsilon\rect\tfrac x\epsilon=\lim_{\epsilon\to0^+}\frac{\epsilon/\pi}{x^2+\epsilon^2}\\&=\lim_{\epsilon\to0^+}\tfrac1{2\epsilon}e^{-|x|/\epsilon}=\lim_{k\to\infty}k\sinc(kx)\end{aligned}\]

    The two exponential representations you must know cold

    (2.45)–(2.46) Delta from an integral of phasors
    \[\delta(x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{-k/2}^{k/2}d\nu\,e^{2\pi i\nu x}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\nu\,e^{2\pi i\nu x}\]
    Phasor picture: at \(x\ne0\) the phasors \(e^{2\pi i\nu x}\) spin all the way around the unit circle as \(\nu\) sweeps, and cancel. At \(x=0\) every phasor points along +Re and they all add. That pile-up is the spike.
    (2.47)–(2.50) The comb
    \[\comb(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty\delta(x-n)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{2\pi inx}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sin\pi Nx}{\sin\pi x}\]
    Replace the integral over \(\nu\) by a sum over integers and you get a delta at every integer, because \(e^{2\pi inx}=1\) for all \(n\) whenever \(x\) is an integer.

    2.2.4 Derivatives of delta

    (2.55)–(2.61)
    \[\begin{gathered}\int f(x)\,\delta^{(n)}(x-x_0)\,dx=(-1)^nf^{(n)}(x_0)\\\delta^{(n)}(-x)=(-1)^n\delta^{(n)}(x)\\ x\,\delta'(x)=-\delta(x)\end{gathered}\]
    Jump discontinuities

    With a symmetric Dirac sequence, \(\int f\,\delta(x-x_0)\,dx=\tfrac12[f(x_0^+)+f(x_0^-)]\) (2.62): the average across the jump. But never use limiting representations for both factors of the same integral; the order of limits matters (the \(h(kx)\) example in §2.3.2 gives only \(f(0^+)\)).

    Delta functions as a basis: \(f(x)=\int da\,f(a)\,\delta(x-a)\) (2.66). The expansion coefficients are just the function values. This is the "space-domain" basis; the Fourier basis is the "frequency-domain" one.

    "Dimension" means three things

    3.1 Sines, cosines and complex exponentials

    The building blocks are \(\cos(2\pi nx/L)\) and \(\sin(2\pi nx/L)\): exactly \(n\) cycles in a length \(L\). The spatial frequency is \(\xi_n=n/L\) cycles per unit length (dimension: length\(^{-1}\)).

    (3.7) One orthogonality relation replaces three
    \[\frac1L\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}dx\;e^{-2\pi inx/L}\,e^{2\pi imx/L}=\delta_{mn}\]
    The sine/cosine versions (3.1)–(3.3) carry an ugly factor of 2 and a special case at \(n=0\). The exponential version has neither, because \(|e^{i\theta}|=1\).
    (3.8) Infinite interval
    \[\int_{-\infty}^\infty dx\,e^{-2\pi i\xi x}e^{2\pi i\xi'x}=\delta(\xi-\xi')\]

    \[\frac1K\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}e^{2\pi i(m-n)k/K}=\delta_{mn}\]
    (3.9)–(3.10) Cosines and sines on \(\R\)
    \[\begin{gathered}2\!\int\!\cos(2\pi\xi x)\cos(2\pi\xi'x)dx=\delta(\xi+\xi')+\delta(\xi-\xi')\\2\!\int\!\sin(2\pi\xi x)\sin(2\pi\xi'x)dx=\delta(\xi-\xi')-\delta(\xi+\xi')\end{gathered}\]

    3.2 Fourier series

    (3.17), (3.19) The pair
    \[f(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty F_n\,e^{2\pi inx/L},\qquad F_n=\frac1L\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}dx\,f(x)\,e^{-2\pi inx/L}\]
    Derivation in one line: multiply the series by \(e^{-2\pi imx/L}\), integrate over one period, use (3.7): only \(n=m\) survives and it contributes \(LF_m\). The \(1/L\) goes on the analysis side; the synthesis side has none.
    (3.14)–(3.16) Real (sin/cos) form
    \[\begin{aligned}F_0&=\tfrac1L\!\int\!f\,dx\\F^{(c)}_n&=\tfrac2L\!\int\!f\cos\tfrac{2\pi nx}L\,dx\\F^{(s)}_n&=\tfrac2L\!\int\!f\sin\tfrac{2\pi nx}L\,dx\end{aligned}\]
    Converting between forms

    For real \(f\): \(F^{(c)}_n=2\Re F_n\), \(F^{(s)}_n=-2\Im F_n\), \(F_0\) = average ("DC term"). Even \(f\) → cosines only (\(F_n\) real, even). Odd \(f\) → sines only (\(F_n\) imaginary, odd).

    Periodicity. Every term has period \(L\), so the series represents either an arbitrary function on one period or a periodic function on all of \(\R\). If \(f(-L/2)\ne f(L/2)\) the periodic extension has a jump at the boundary. You can integrate over any full period (3.21).

    3.2.3 Properties of the coefficients

    • Linearity: \(h=\alpha f+\beta g\Rightarrow H_n=\alpha F_n+\beta G_n\).
    • Real \(f\): \(F_n^*=F_{-n}\) (3.45). B&M call this "Hermiticity"; it is conjugate symmetry, not a Hermitian operator.
    • Decay: \(F_n\to0\); if \(f\) has \(q\) continuous derivatives, \(n^qF_n\to0\) (3.49). Jumps ⇒ \(1/n\); kinks ⇒ \(1/n^2\).
    • Derivative: coefficients of \(f'\) are \(\frac{2\pi in}{L}F_n\) (3.54). Integration divides by \(2\pi in/L\) and always helps convergence.
    (3.50)–(3.51) Parseval for series
    \[\sum_n|F_n|^2=\frac1L\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}|f(x)|^2dx,\qquad\sum_nF_n^*G_n=\frac1L\int f^*(x)g(x)\,dx\]
    The map \(f\mapsto\{F_n\}\) is unitary except for the stray factor \(1/L\) (the orthonormal basis is \(\frac1{\sqrt L}e^{2\pi inx/L}\), but \(F_n\) uses \(\frac1L\)).
    Worked example: square wave \(f=\rect(2x/L)\) on \([-L/2,L/2)\)

    \(F_n=\frac1L\int_{-L/4}^{L/4}e^{-2\pi inx/L}dx=\frac1L\cdot\frac{e^{-i\pi n/2}-e^{i\pi n/2}}{-2\pi in/L}=\frac{\sin(\pi n/2)}{\pi n}=\tfrac12\sinc(n/2)\).

    So \(F_0=\tfrac12\), \(F_{\pm1}=\tfrac1\pi\), \(F_{\pm2}=0\), \(F_{\pm3}=-\tfrac1{3\pi}\)… Real and even because \(f\) is real and even; \(1/n\) decay because \(f\) jumps. Compare §3.3.8: \(F_n=\frac1LF_0(n/L)\) with \(F_0(\xi)=\frac L2\sinc(\frac L2\xi)\). ✓

    Derivative check: \(f'=\delta(x+L/4)-\delta(x-L/4)\) has coefficients \(\frac{2\pi in}{L}\cdot\frac{\sin(\pi n/2)}{\pi n}=\frac{2i}{L}\sin\frac{\pi n}2\), matching (3.55).

    Laurent connection. With \(w=e^{2\pi ix/L}\) on the unit circle, the Fourier series is a Laurent series \(\sum F_nw^n\), and \(F_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f_w(w)}{w^{n+1}}dw\) (3.25). The unit circle in the \(w\)-plane is one period of \(x\).

    3.3 The one-dimensional Fourier transform

    Let \(L\to\infty\): \(n/L\to\xi\), \(\Delta\xi=1/L\), and \(F_n/\Delta\xi\to F(\xi)\).

    (3.60)–(3.61) B&M convention: \(2\pi\) in the exponent, no prefactors
    \[F(\xi)=\mathcal F_1\{f\}=\int_{-\infty}^\infty dx\,f(x)\,e^{-2\pi i\xi x},\qquad f(x)=\mathcal F_1^{-1}\{F\}=\int_{-\infty}^\infty d\xi\,F(\xi)\,e^{+2\pi i\xi x}\]
    Phasor reading: \(F(\xi)\) asks "how much of the spinning phasor \(e^{2\pi i\xi x}\) is in \(f\)?" by multiplying by the counter-rotating phasor and adding everything up. If \(f\) contains that rotation, the product stops spinning and accumulates; otherwise it cancels.
    Convention check

    Engineering books with \(\omega=2\pi\xi\) put \(1/2\pi\) in front of the inverse. B&M never do. If you write \(e^{-i\omega x}\), every scaling result picks up \(2\pi\)'s. Stay in \(\xi\) (cycles/length) on the exam.

    3.3.6 Convolution and correlation

    (3.114) Convolution · (3.115) correlation
    \[\begin{aligned}[f*h](x)&=\int dx'\,f(x')\,h(x-x')\\ [f\star h](x)&=\int dx'\,f(x+x')\,h(x')=\int dx''\,f(x'')\,h(x''-x)\end{aligned}\]
    Flip, shift, multiply, integrate. Convolution is commutative, associative, bilinear, and commutes with shifts and derivatives: \((f*g)'=f'*g=f*g'\). Correlation is not commutative and has no flip: \(f\star h=f(x)*h(-x)\).
    (3.132) Convolution theorem
    \[\mathcal F\{f*h\}=F(\xi)\,H(\xi)\qquad\text{and dually}\qquad\mathcal F\{f\,h\}=F*H\]
    Proof idea: write both functions as inverse transforms, do the \(x'\) integral first, get \(\delta(\xi-\xi')\) from (3.8). Valid when at most one factor is a generalized function of infinite support.
    Scale factors inside convolutions (3.143)–(3.146)

    \(\rect(x)*\rect(x)=\tri(x)\), but \(\rect(x/L)*\rect(x/L)=L\,\tri(x/L)\), not \(\tri(x/L)\). Convolution is an integral over \(x\), so it carries a dimension of length. Safest habit: define \(r_L(x)=\rect(x/L)\) and write the integral out. In expressions like \(f(x)*h(x/b)\), the shift variable is the unscaled \(x\).

    Convolution with deltas
    • \(f*\delta=f\) (identity) · \(f(x)*\delta(x-x_0)=f(x-x_0)\) (shift) · \(f*\delta'=f'\) (no minus sign) (3.126)
    • \(\delta(x-x_1)*\delta(x-x_2)=\delta(x-x_1-x_2)\) · \(\delta^{(m)}*\delta^{(n)}=\delta^{(m+n)}\)
    • Area rule: \(\int(f*h)=\int f\int h\).

    Periodic in \(x\) ⇔ discrete in \(\xi\). And the series coefficients are samples of the one-period transform: \(F_n=\frac1LF_0(n/L)\). This is the fastest way to get Fourier-series coefficients on an exam.
    (3.197)–(3.198) Poisson summation
    \[\sum_n g(x-nL)=\frac1L\sum_kG\!\Big(\frac kL\Big)e^{2\pi ikx/L},\qquad\sum_ng(nL)=\frac1L\sum_kG\!\Big(\frac kL\Big)\]
    The sum of samples of a function equals the (scaled) sum of samples of its transform. Here the replicas may overlap; \(g\) need not be periodic or compact.
    Example: \(\sum_n e^{-\pi n^2t}\) via Poisson

    Take \(g(x)=e^{-\pi tx^2}=\gaus(\sqrt t\,x)\), so \(G(\xi)=t^{-1/2}e^{-\pi\xi^2/t}\). With \(L=1\): \(\sum_ne^{-\pi tn^2}=t^{-1/2}\sum_ke^{-\pi k^2/t}\) (the theta-function identity; compare 3.199). Slowly converging on one side means fast on the other.

    3.3.9–3.3.10 Analyticity, cosine and sine transforms

    Paley–Wiener: if \(f\) has compact support, \(F(\xi)\) extends to an entire function of complex \(\xi\) (of exponential type). An analytic function zero on an interval is zero everywhere, so a space-limited function is never band-limited. Cosine transform \(F_c(\xi)=2\int_0^\infty f\cos(2\pi\xi x)dx\) (even extension); sine transform \(F_s=2\int_0^\infty f\sin(2\pi\xi x)dx\) (odd extension); each inverts with the same kernel for \(x\ge0\).

    3.5 Sampling theory

    A function is bandlimited if \(F(\xi)=0\) for \(|\xi|>B/2\). B&M's \(B\) is the full bandwidth (positive and negative frequencies); engineers often call \(B/2\) the bandwidth. Bandlimited functions are entire, and they form Paley–Wiener space, a reproducing-kernel space with kernel \(B\sinc(Bx)\).

    (3.288)–(3.290) Sampling = multiplying by a comb = replicating the spectrum
    \[\begin{aligned}f_s(x)&=f(x)\,\frac1{\Delta x}\comb\Big(\frac x{\Delta x}\Big)=\sum_jf(j\Delta x)\,\delta(x-j\Delta x)\\ \Longrightarrow\quad F_s(\xi)&=F(\xi)*\comb(\Delta x\,\xi)=\frac1{\Delta x}\sum_jF\Big(\xi-\frac j{\Delta x}\Big)\end{aligned}\]
    (3.291) Nyquist · (3.293) Whittaker–Shannon interpolation
    \[\Delta x\le\frac1B\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f(x)=\sum_jf(j\Delta x)\,\sinc[B(x-j\Delta x)]\quad(B\Delta x\le1)\]
    Replicas sit \(1/\Delta x\) apart and each is \(B\) wide, so they don't overlap if \(1/\Delta x\ge B\). Cut one replica out with \(\Delta x\,\rect(\xi/B)\); in space that is convolution with \(B\Delta x\sinc(Bx)\). With \(B\Delta x=1\): \(f(x)=\sum f(k/B)\sinc(Bx-k)\) (3.287).
    • Aliasing = heterodyning: multiplying by the comb's harmonics shifts every frequency to \(\xi\pm n/\Delta x\). Equivalently, \(\cos2\pi\xi x-\cos2\pi(\xi\pm n/\Delta x)x\) is a null function of the sampling operator. Cures: sample finer, or low-pass (anti-alias) filter before sampling.
    • Dual Nyquist (3.296): if \(f\) is space-limited to width \(L\), samples of \(F\) spaced \(\Delta\xi\le1/L\) determine it: \(F(\xi)=\sum F(j\Delta\xi)\sinc(\xi/\Delta\xi-j)\).
    • Space-bandwidth product \(LB\): number of samples (degrees of freedom) for a signal of length \(L\) and bandwidth \(B\).
    • Multi-D (3.303): sample on lattice \(\mathbf P\mathbf m\); replicas sit on the reciprocal lattice \(\mathbf Q\mathbf m\); interpolate with \(|\det\mathbf P|\,s(\mathbf r)\), the inverse FT of the support function.
    • Finite aperture (3.306)–(3.310): \(f_j=[f*a](x_j)\). Recoverable if \(A(\xi)\ne0\) in the band; pixel apertures of width \(\epsilon<2\Delta x\) are fine.

    What a DFT really computes (Fig. 3.10)

    The DFT of the samples of \(f\) gives the samples of the Fourier transform of a sampled, windowed, and periodically replicated version of \(f\). Each step has an error and a cure:

    • Sampling → aliasing. Cure: finer \(\Delta x\) or anti-alias filter.
    • Windowing to \([0,L)\) → convolution with \(W(\xi)\) = spectral leakage. Cure: wider or smoother window.
    • Sampling \(F\) at \(1/L\) → coarse spectrum. Cure: zero padding (longer \(L\), finer \(\Delta\xi\); adds no information).

    3.6.4 Discrete-space FT

    (3.342)–(3.343)
    \[F_{DS}(\xi)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty f_k\,e^{-2\pi ik\xi\Delta x},\qquad f_k=\Delta x\int_{-1/2\Delta x}^{1/2\Delta x}d\xi\,F_{DS}(\xi)\,e^{2\pi ik\xi\Delta x}\]
    A Fourier series in the frequency variable, period \(1/\Delta x\). \(F_{DS}=F_s=\frac1{\Delta x}\sum F(\xi-n/\Delta x)\): the replicated spectrum of row 3 of Fig. 3.10. Setting \(\xi=0\) recovers Poisson summation.

    3.6.5 FFT

    (3.356)–(3.357) Danielson–Lanczos split, \(N=2M\)
    \[F_n=F_n^{(e)}+W_{2M}^n\,F_n^{(o)},\qquad F_{n+M}=F_n^{(e)}-W_{2M}^n\,F_n^{(o)}\]
    Split into even- and odd-indexed samples, each an \(M\)-point DFT. The minus sign comes from \(W_{2M}^M=e^{-i\pi}=-1\): half a turn on the unit circle. Repeat \(\log_2N\) times: \(O(N\log_2N)\) instead of \(O(N^2)\).

    2D DFT: \(F_{mn}=\sum_j\sum_kf_{jk}W_N^{mj+nk}\), i.e. \(\mathbf F=\mathbf D\mathbf f\mathbf D\) (not a similarity transform). Do 1D DFTs on columns then rows: \(O(N^2\log_2N^2)\). Lexicographic ordering \(\ell=k+jN\) turns it into one \(N^2\times N^2\) matrix.

    Chapter 7

    Deterministic Descriptions of Imaging Systems

    Now the abstract machinery describes real hardware. Objects and images are vectors, systems are operators, and the SVD of a shift-invariant system turns out to be the Fourier transform.

    7.1 Objects and images

    An object is a function \(f(\mathbf r)\) of a \(q\)-D vector (space, plus time, wavelength, angle as needed); it may be complex (coherent fields \(A(\mathbf r)e^{i\phi(\mathbf r)}\)) or vector-valued (RGB, MRI's three maps). Real objects have compact support and finite values, so they are square-integrable: \(\mathbf f\in\L_2(\mathbf S_f)\). Don't assume objects are smooth or bandlimited; edges exist.

    (7.13)–(7.15) Fourier basis for a supported object
    \[f(\mathbf r)=\sum_{\mathbf k}F_{\mathbf k}\,\Phi_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r),\qquad\Phi_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)=e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol\rho_{\mathbf k}\cdot\mathbf r}\,S_f(\mathbf r),\qquad\boldsymbol\rho_{\mathbf k}=\mathbf k\,\Delta\rho,\ \Delta\rho\le1/L\]
    \(\mathbf k\) is a multi-index (a vector of integers). The support function \(S_f\) zeros all periodic replicas but one. Truncating the sum to finitely many terms gives a finite representation.
    (7.27), (7.37) General finite representation
    \[f_a(\mathbf r)=\sum_{n=1}^N\theta_n\phi_n(\mathbf r),\qquad\boldsymbol\theta=\mathcal D_\chi f,\quad f_a=\mathcal D_\phi^\dagger\mathcal D_\chi f\]
    Two choices: expansion functions \(\phi_n\) (pixels, voxels, blobs) and coefficient rule \(\chi_n\) (average over the pixel, or point sample). Best \(L_2\) accuracy: \(\mathcal D_\phi^\dagger\mathcal D_\chi=\Pc_{rep}\), the projector onto representation space; then \(\|\delta f\|^2=\|f\|^2-\|\boldsymbol\theta\|^2\) for orthonormal \(\phi_n\).
    • Karhunen–Loève: over an ensemble, the eigenvectors of the autocorrelation operator \(\mathcal R\) minimize mean-square representation error; MSRE \(=\sum_{n>N}\lambda_n\).
    • Moment error: \(\delta\tau=\int f\,[\Ic-\Pc_{rep}]t\). Only the template \(t\) needs to be representable: integrals over whole pixels are exact.
    • Uniform translates \(\phi(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_{\mathbf n})\): aliasing terms appear in the error unless \(\phi\) or \(f\) is nearly bandlimited; non-orthogonal translates need a biorthonormal set built with the DFT.
    • Nonlinear parametric models: point sources, shapes, atlas warping, thresholded wavelets.

    7.2 Linear continuous-to-continuous systems

    (7.101) The general linear CC system
    \[g(\mathbf r_d)=\int_{\mathbf S_f}d^qr\;h(\mathbf r_d,\mathbf r)\,f(\mathbf r)\]
    Put in a point source \(f=\delta(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)\) and sifting gives \(g(\mathbf r_d)=h(\mathbf r_d,\mathbf r_0)\). So the kernel is the point response function (PRF): the image of a point at \(\mathbf r_0\). For a general system it changes shape with \(\mathbf r_0\).
    • FWHM \(\delta_{FWHM}(x_0)=x_{d+}-x_{d-}\) where \(h\) falls to half its peak. Other widths: variance of normalized PRF (7.110), Rayleigh (peak to first zero).
    • Flood image \(g_{fld}(\mathbf r_d)=\int h(\mathbf r_d,\mathbf r)\,d^qr\) (uniform object in). Normalize images by it.
    • Point sensitivity \(s_{pt}(\mathbf r_0)=\int h(\mathbf r_d,\mathbf r_0)\,d^sr_d\) (total signal from a point). Distortion can ruin the flood image while leaving \(s_{pt}\) constant.
    (7.114) Adjoint = backprojection · (7.116) kernel of \(\Hc^\dagger\Hc\)
    \[[\Hc^\dagger g](\mathbf r)=\int d^sr_d\,g(\mathbf r_d)\,h^*(\mathbf r_d,\mathbf r),\qquad k(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=\int d^sr_d\,h^*(\mathbf r_d,\mathbf r)\,h(\mathbf r_d,\mathbf r')\]
    For a real blurring kernel, \(\Hc^\dagger\) blurs again and \(\Hc^\dagger\Hc\) blurs twice. SVD then follows Ch. 1: \(\beta_n=\sqrt{\mu_n}\,\alpha_n\) (7.129).

    7.2.3 Shift-invariant systems

    (7.133)–(7.136)
    \[h(\mathbf r_d,\mathbf r)=h(\mathbf r_d-\mathbf r)\ \Rightarrow\ g=h*f,\qquad[\Hc^\dagger g](\mathbf r)=\int d^qr_d\,h^*(\mathbf r_d-\mathbf r)g(\mathbf r_d)=[g\star h^*](\mathbf r)\]
    Now the kernel is the point spread function (PSF), the same shape everywhere. Adjoint of convolution = correlation with \(h^*\). Hermitian iff \(h(-\mathbf r)=h^*(\mathbf r)\). Flood image and point sensitivity are automatically constant (7.137).
    Temporal vs. spatial filters

    Electrical filters are causal: \(p(t,t')=0\) for \(t<t'\). Spatial PSFs need not be. True spatial shift invariance is rare (lens axes and fields of view break it) but linear scanners and free-space diffraction are genuinely LSIV.

    7.2.4–7.2.5 Eigenfunctions and SVD of a convolution

    1. Convolution fails Hilbert–Schmidt (7.138) ⇒ expect a continuous spectrum.
    2. Fourier-transform the eigen-equation \(h*\psi=\lambda\psi\): \(H(\boldsymbol\rho)\Psi(\boldsymbol\rho)=\lambda\Psi(\boldsymbol\rho)\). The only function that survives multiplication by \(H\) unchanged in shape is a delta: \(\Psi=\delta(\boldsymbol\rho-\boldsymbol\rho_0)\), since \(H(\boldsymbol\rho)\delta(\boldsymbol\rho-\boldsymbol\rho_0)=H(\boldsymbol\rho_0)\delta(\boldsymbol\rho-\boldsymbol\rho_0)\).
    3. Invert: the eigenfunctions are plane waves and the eigenvalues are the transfer function.
    (7.143)–(7.149)
    \[\psi_{\boldsymbol\rho}(\mathbf r)=e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol\rho\cdot\mathbf r},\qquad\lambda_{\boldsymbol\rho}=H(\boldsymbol\rho),\qquad\sqrt{\mu_{\boldsymbol\rho}}=|H(\boldsymbol\rho)|,\qquad v_{\boldsymbol\rho}(\mathbf r_d)=\frac{H(\boldsymbol\rho)}{|H(\boldsymbol\rho)|}e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol\rho\cdot\mathbf r_d}\]
    Direct check (7.145): \(\int h(\mathbf r)e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol\rho\cdot(\mathbf r_d-\mathbf r)}d\mathbf r=e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol\rho\cdot\mathbf r_d}\int h(\mathbf r)e^{-2\pi i\boldsymbol\rho\cdot\mathbf r}d\mathbf r=H(\boldsymbol\rho)\,e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol\rho\cdot\mathbf r_d}\). A phasor goes in; the same phasor comes out, scaled by \(|H|\) and rotated by \(\arg H\).

    7.2.6 Transfer functions

    (7.154)–(7.157), (7.163)
    \[\text{OTF}(\boldsymbol\rho)=\frac{H(\boldsymbol\rho)}{H(\mathbf 0)},\qquad\text{MTF}(\boldsymbol\rho)=\frac{|H(\boldsymbol\rho)|}{H(\mathbf 0)},\qquad M=\frac{f_{max}-f_{min}}{f_{max}+f_{min}},\qquad\text{MTF}=\frac{M_g}{M_f}\]
    Feed in \(A+B\cos(2\pi\boldsymbol\rho\cdot\mathbf r-\phi_f)\). Output (7.161): \(AH(\mathbf 0)+B|H(\boldsymbol\rho)|\cos[2\pi\boldsymbol\rho\cdot\mathbf r_d-\phi_f+\Phi_H(\boldsymbol\rho)]\). Amplitude drops by MTF; the cosine shifts by the phase transfer function \(\Phi_H=\arg H\). Real \(h\) ⇒ \(H(-\boldsymbol\rho)=H^*(\boldsymbol\rho)\) ("conjugate symmetry"), so \(H(\mathbf 0)\) is real.

    7.2.7 Magnifiers

    (7.172)–(7.179)
    \[\begin{gathered}h=\delta(\mathbf r_d-m\mathbf r)\ \Rightarrow\ g(\mathbf r_d)=|m|^{-q}f(\mathbf r_d/m);\\ h=h(\mathbf r_d-m\mathbf r)\ \Rightarrow\ \mu_{\boldsymbol\rho}=|m|^{-q}|H(\boldsymbol\rho/m)|^2\end{gathered}\]
    Magnification breaks shift-invariance of \(\Hc\), but \(\Hc^\dagger\Hc\) is still LSIV, so plane waves remain the object-space singular functions. Rescale image coordinates by \(1/m\) and it becomes an ordinary convolution. Affine map \(\mathbf r_d=\mathbf M\mathbf r+\mathbf r_{d0}\): replace \(|m|^q\) with \(|\det\mathbf M|\). Shift-variant magnification \(\mathbf M(\mathbf r)\): delta functions become the SVD basis, \(\mu_{\mathbf a}=1/|\det\mathbf M(\mathbf a)|\), and the flood image is nonuniform.

    7.2.8 Approximately shift-invariant systems

    • Finite field of view \(h(\mathbf r_d-\mathbf r)b_g(\mathbf r_d)b_f(\mathbf r)\): truncated plane waves \(b_{tot}(\mathbf r)e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol\rho\cdot\mathbf r}\) are approximate singular functions if \(H\) varies slowly on the scale \(1/L\). Errors stay within about one resolution width of the edge.
    • Weakly shift-variant \(p(\mathbf r_d-\mathbf r_0;\mathbf r_0)\), with \(\delta_{FWHM}\ll\delta_b\ll\Delta\): use a local (windowed, Gabor) Fourier transform; \(P(\boldsymbol\rho;\mathbf r_0)\) acts as a local transfer function (7.200).

    7.2.9–7.2.10 Rotational symmetry and axial systems

    If every rotation \(\mathcal T_\phi\) commutes with \(\Hc^\dagger\Hc\), its eigenfunctions are \(u_{jk}(r)e^{ik\theta}\): angular phasors, one for each irreducible representation of the rotation group \(C_\infty\). The kernel depends only on \(r,r'\) and \(\theta-\theta'\). Mirror symmetry pairs \(e^{\pm ik\theta}\) into doubly degenerate eigenvalues (group \(D_\infty\)). Rotation + translation ⇒ infinite degeneracy: all \(\boldsymbol\rho\) on a circle share \(|H(\rho)|^2\), and the Jacobi–Anger expansion \(e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol\rho\cdot\mathbf r}=\sum_ki^kJ_k(2\pi\rho r)\,e^{ik(\theta-\theta_\rho)}\) converts between the plane-wave basis and the angular one (cf. 7.216).

    Axial 3D→2D systems \(g(\mathbf r_d)=\int d^2r\int dz\,h(\mathbf r_d-\mathbf r;z)f(\mathbf r,z)\): only one nonzero singular value per lateral frequency, with \(u_{\boldsymbol\rho,1}\propto e^{2\pi i\boldsymbol\rho\cdot\mathbf r}H(-\boldsymbol\rho;z)\) and \(\mu_{\boldsymbol\rho,1}=\int dz\,|H(\boldsymbol\rho;z)|^2\). Everything else is null space: depth information is mostly invisible.

    7.3 Continuous-to-discrete systems upcoming

    (7.225), (7.230), (7.237)
    \[\begin{gathered}g_m=\int d^qr\,h_m(\mathbf r)f(\mathbf r)\\ h_m(\mathbf r)=\int d^sr_d\,w_m(\mathbf r_d)h(\mathbf r_d,\mathbf r)\\ {}[\Hc^\dagger\mathbf g](\mathbf r)=\sum_mg_mh_m^*(\mathbf r)\end{gathered}\]
    A digital camera = continuous blur then pixel integration. \(\Hc\Hc^\dagger\) is an \(M\times M\) matrix with entries \(\int h_mh_k^*\); at most \(M\) nonzero singular values, so the null space is always infinite-dimensional.

    Fourier crosstalk matrix \(\beta_{\mathbf k\mathbf k'}=(\Hc\Phi_{\mathbf k},\Hc\Phi_{\mathbf k'})\) generalizes the transfer function to sampled systems: diagonal entries measure how strongly frequency \(\mathbf k\) gets through; off-diagonal entries measure aliasing. For an LSIV system sampled at \(M\) points, \(\beta_{\mathbf k\mathbf k}=M|P(\boldsymbol\rho_{\mathbf k})|^2\) and \(|\cos\theta_{kk'}|=\big|\frac{\sin[\pi M\epsilon(\xi_k-\xi_{k'})]}{M\sin[\pi\epsilon(\xi_k-\xi_{k'})]}\big|\), the Dirichlet kernel again: full aliasing when \(\xi_k-\xi_{k'}\) is a multiple of \(1/\epsilon\).
